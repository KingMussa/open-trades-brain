# Field Sketching & One-Line Maps — Make Your Own Blueprints

*When the drawing set is missing, outdated, or too dense — draw your own. A good field sketch is a working document.*

## Why Make Your Own

- One-line diagrams make a whole system understandable at a glance (design drawings show everything; a one-line shows the IDEA)
- Zone/area maps you draw yourself match how YOU navigate the site
- Sketching a system forces you to actually understand flow direction, isolation points, and sequence — the best way to learn a building
- Marked-up sketches are how you communicate tie-ins, MOPs, and shutdowns to crews and inspectors

## The One-Line Method (hydronic example)

1. **Start at the source** (chillers/boilers) — draw one box per machine, labeled with tag + key spec (tons/HP)
2. **Draw flow direction with arrows** — supply one color/direction, return the other
3. **Add the big pieces in order:** primary pumps → headers → secondary pumps/CDU → distribution loops → terminal equipment (CRAH/FAU rows)
4. **Mark every isolation point** with its valve tag — this turns the sketch into a shutdown planning tool
5. **Mark test/gauge points** (PT ports, thermometers, flow meters) — your diagnostic access
6. **Note sizes on mains** and flow (GPM) where known
One page per system. If it needs two pages, make a key plan showing how they join.

## Zone / Area Maps

- Base it on the grid system: draw grid lines, label columns, then place equipment tags in their bays
- Mark: equipment tags, valve clusters, panels (electrical + DDC), drains, access doors, rigging paths
- Keep one per level/sector in your notebook or app — update in pencil, publish in ink

## Rules for Sketches That Get Used

- Date + initial every sketch; note the source (which drawings/field walk it came from)
- Label NORTH or a known landmark — a map without orientation is art, not a tool
- Sketch to communicate, not to impress: boxes, lines, arrows, tags. Nobody grades your drafting
- Photo + markup beats fresh drawing for existing conditions: snap the area, draw on the photo
- Verify against reality on your next walk — a wrong sketch is worse than none; kill or fix bad info fast
- Keep a revision habit like the real drawings: when the system changes, rev the sketch
